# Disclosure Decision Framework

> **This is general guidance, not legal or medical advice. For specific decisions about your application, accommodation, or disclosure, consult an employment advocate, disability-rights lawyer, or — where relevant — a clinician.**

---

## How to use this document

This document is a guided worksheet. It lays out a default position, a set of factors that may shift that default, and some practical language you can adapt. It does not make the decision for you. Every disclosure situation is shaped by context — the employer, the role, the jurisdiction, the timing, your own values and risk tolerance. This framework helps you think through those layers. You decide.

Identity-first language is used throughout (for example, "autistic person" rather than "person with autism") because that reflects the most widely expressed preference of neurodivergent communities. If you prefer person-first language, or a different framing entirely, that preference takes priority.

---

## Default position: do not disclose on the resume

The starting point in this framework is **not to disclose neurodivergent identity on the resume itself.**

This is not a statement about whether disclosure is wrong or right. It is a practical starting point grounded in three observations about how the hiring process typically works.

- **Accommodations are protected separately from resume content in most jurisdictions.** Disability and accessibility law in many places provides for reasonable accommodation requests at the application and employment stages, independent of whether you disclosed anything on your resume. In many jurisdictions, you do not need to disclose on the resume in order to request a reasonable accommodation later in the process.

- **Disclosure on a resume cannot be retracted.** Once that information is on a document, every reader of every version of that resume has access to it. The decision to include it is not reversible in the way that a verbal disclosure in a specific conversation might be. That asymmetry is worth weighing.

- **Later-stage disclosure tends to carry stronger protections in many jurisdictions.** Disclosure at the interview stage, after an offer, or after acceptance generally occurs when there is more legal standing and, in some cases, more contextual grounding for the conversation. The specifics vary significantly by jurisdiction and circumstance — this is one reason why consulting an employment advocate or disability-rights lawyer for your specific situation matters.

None of this means you should not disclose on your resume. It means the default starting point is caution, and that any shift toward earlier disclosure deserves deliberate thought.

## Six factors that may shift the default toward disclosure

Work through these questions honestly. If one or more of these applies to your situation, it may be reasonable to move closer to neutral signalling or explicit disclosure. None of them is a directive.

**1. Is the employer ND-affirming in concrete, formal-program terms — not just marketing copy?**

There is a meaningful difference between an employer who uses ND-friendly language in job postings and one who has a documented, structured neurodivergent hiring initiative, ND-affiliated leadership, or contractual diversity commitments with accountability attached. If you can verify the latter, the risk profile of early disclosure changes. If the affirming framing lives only in marketing copy, the default position holds.

**2. Is the role itself disability, accessibility, or ND-affirming-adjacent?**

Some roles — accessibility researcher, ND community liaison, inclusive-design lead, disability-focused programme manager — have a structural relationship with disability identity that may make disclosure feel more contextually natural and lower-risk. The employer's investment in these roles often signals a different institutional relationship to disclosure than a generic corporate hiring process.

**3. Are you applying through a channel that already screens for ND inclusion?**

Some recruiting channels — community-led hiring programmes, ND-targeted recruiter networks, disability-focused career fairs — are structured so that the ND inclusion context is already established before any individual application arrives. Disclosure through a channel designed for it carries different risk than disclosure into a generic application portal.

**4. Do you need an accommodation at the application stage itself?**

If the application process requires something you cannot access without accommodation — a modified interview format, written questions instead of a phone screen, advance sharing of interview questions, a sensory-aware location — some form of disclosure may be necessary to request that accommodation. In this case, the question of whether to disclose is partly answered by the question of what you need in order to participate fairly.

**5. Are you values-driven to disclose regardless of bias risk?**

Some people hold the position that they would rather not work for an employer who would screen them out because of their neurodivergent identity — and that early disclosure is, in effect, a filter they are applying to the employer. This is a coherent and legitimate position. If this describes you, the framework shifts: the question becomes not whether to disclose but how and when.

**6. Has an employment advocate or disability-rights lawyer advised disclosure in your specific case?**

If you have consulted a professional with knowledge of your jurisdiction, your specific circumstances, and the applicable law, and they have advised disclosure at the resume or early application stage, that advice carries weight that a general framework like this one cannot match. Professional, case-specific guidance is the most reliable basis for a disclosure decision.

## Three disclosure strengths

These are not ranked options. They are different approaches with different tradeoffs. Choose the one that fits your situation, values, and risk tolerance.

### Non-disclosure

**Definition:** The resume contains no reference to neurodivergent identity, disability, or accessibility needs. The applicant reserves all disclosure decisions for later in the process.

**Example sentence (cover letter, where relevant):**
"I am particularly interested in roles where systematic thinking and depth of focus are valued — I bring both in sustained quantity."

This language signals a characteristic without naming an identity. It is not disclosure. It is honest framing of a strength.

---

### Neutral signalling

**Definition:** The resume or cover letter includes language that reflects neurodivergent-associated strengths or accessibility-aware professional experience without explicitly naming neurodivergent identity. This is the **BRAINS skill recommended path** when there is values alignment with the employer but uncertainty about bias risk.

**Example sentence (resume skills or profile section):**
"Accessibility-aware UX research: experience designing for and with neurodivergent users, applying cognitive-load and sensory-accessibility principles to research protocols."

This names a professional competency. It is not an identity disclosure. A reader who is ND-aware may infer alignment; a reader who is not simply reads a skill.

---

### Explicit disclosure

**Definition:** The resume or cover letter directly names the applicant's neurodivergent identity as part of their professional narrative.

**Example sentence (cover letter):**
"As an autistic researcher, I have built my practice around the kind of pattern-recognition and systematic analysis that your team's work depends on — and I bring direct lived understanding of the user populations you serve."

Explicit disclosure is appropriate when the factors above point toward it, when the applicant has made a deliberate values-based decision, or when professional advice supports it. It should be used with full awareness that it cannot be retracted.

## Talking points for downstream disclosure

These are non-prescriptive templates. They are starting points, not scripts. Adapt them to your own voice, to the specific employer, and to whatever you know about the culture and people you will be speaking with. The exact moment and phrasing of any disclosure conversation belongs to you.

### At the interview stage

> "I want to share something that I think is relevant to how I work and how I'd like to set up this conversation for success. I'm [autistic / an ADHDer / neurodivergent — use your own framing]. That shapes how I process and communicate, and I find I do my best work when [brief, specific note on what helps — e.g., 'I have a moment to gather my thoughts before answering a complex question']. I'm happy to say more about that if it's useful."

This keeps the disclosure brief, grounds it in practical context, and opens a door without requiring the interviewer to know what to do with it.

### After an offer

> "Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role. Before I formally accept, I'd like to share something and, if appropriate, discuss whether any accommodations would be helpful going forward. I'm [neurodivergent identity in your preferred terms]. I don't anticipate this affecting my performance in the ways the role requires, but I want to be open about it and explore whether there's anything that would help me do my best work from the start."

Post-offer disclosure often carries a different legal and interpersonal weight than pre-offer disclosure. In many jurisdictions, an offer cannot legally be withdrawn on the basis of a disability disclosure — though the specifics vary, and professional advice applies here.

### After acceptance — when requesting an accommodation or during onboarding

> "I want to raise something as we set up how I'll be working. I'm [neurodivergent identity], and I've found that [specific, practical accommodation — e.g., 'having tasks communicated in writing rather than verbally' / 'a quiet workspace for focused work'] makes a real difference to the quality and consistency of my output. I'd like to explore whether that's something we can put in place. I'm happy to walk through what I need and why, at whatever level of detail is helpful."

This framing is specific and practical. It focuses on what will enable good work, which is also what accommodation law in many jurisdictions centres on. A disability-rights lawyer or employment advocate can advise on the formal accommodation request process for your jurisdiction.
