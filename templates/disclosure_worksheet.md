## Default position

The starting point in this framework is **not to disclose neurodivergent identity on the resume itself.** This is not a statement about whether disclosure is wrong or right. It is a practical starting point grounded in three observations:

- **Accommodations are protected separately from resume content in most jurisdictions.** In many places you can request reasonable accommodation later without disclosing on the resume.
- **Disclosure on a resume cannot be retracted.** Once it is on a document, every reader of every version has access to it. The decision is not reversible the way a verbal disclosure in a specific conversation might be.
- **Later-stage disclosure tends to carry stronger protections.** Disclosure at interview, post-offer, or post-acceptance generally occurs when there is more legal standing and contextual grounding. Specifics vary significantly by jurisdiction.

The default holds unless the six factors below shift it.

The framework's three disclosure-strength definitions, for reference:

- **Non-disclosure** — the resume contains no reference to neurodivergent identity, disability, or accessibility needs. All disclosure decisions are reserved for later in the process.
- **Neutral signalling** — language reflects ND-associated strengths or accessibility-aware professional experience without explicitly naming neurodivergent identity. A reader who is ND-aware may infer alignment; a reader who is not simply reads a competency.
- **Explicit disclosure** — the resume or cover letter directly names neurodivergent identity as part of the professional narrative. Used with full awareness that it cannot be retracted.

## Talking points for downstream disclosure (reference)

If you go with non-disclosure or neutral signalling on the resume, the question of how to talk about identity at interview and after an offer still arises. These templates are starting points, not scripts. Adapt them to your own voice.

**At interview stage** (illustrative — adapt freely):

> "I want to share something I think is relevant to how I work and how I'd like to set this conversation up for success. I'm [autistic / an ADHDer / neurodivergent — your framing]. That shapes how I process and communicate, and I do my best work when [specific note — e.g., 'I have a moment to gather my thoughts before answering a complex question']. Happy to say more if it's useful."

**After an offer** (illustrative — adapt freely):

> "Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role. Before I formally accept I'd like to share something and, if appropriate, discuss whether any accommodations would be helpful. I'm [neurodivergent identity in your preferred terms]. I don't anticipate this affecting performance in the ways the role requires, but I want to be open about it and explore whether there's anything that would help me do my best work from the start."

**After acceptance — requesting an accommodation** (illustrative — adapt freely):

> "I want to raise something as we set up how I'll be working. I'm [neurodivergent identity], and I've found that [specific, practical accommodation — e.g., 'having tasks communicated in writing rather than verbally' / 'a quiet workspace for focused work'] makes a real difference to the quality and consistency of my output. I'd like to explore whether that's something we can put in place."

Post-offer disclosure often carries different legal and interpersonal weight than pre-offer disclosure. In many jurisdictions an offer cannot legally be withdrawn on the basis of a disability disclosure — though specifics vary, and a disability-rights lawyer or employment advocate can speak to your situation.
